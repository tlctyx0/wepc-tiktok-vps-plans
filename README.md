## How much upload bandwidth a TikTok live stream actually needs

| Streaming setup | Minimum | Recommended | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Phone live | 3Mbps | 5Mbps+ | Single account, light streaming |
| OBS desktop push | 5Mbps | 10Mbps+ | Multi-scene, selling streams |
| OBS 1080p | 10Mbps | 20Mbps+ | Brand studios |
| Multiple accounts in parallel | 20Mbps+ | 50Mbps+ | Agency matrices |

TikTok's video bitrate range runs roughly 516Kbps to 25Mbps, with a practical average near 2Mbps for everyday pushes. Size the port at twice your target bitrate so network jitter has somewhere to go. At 5Mbps you burn about 2.25GB per hour, which means the 1TB traffic allowance on the cheaper plans is not the limit you will hit first.

Against those benchmarks, most of WePC's catalogue is over-provisioned in the right way. The 200Mbps ports are ten times the 1080p recommendation. The three 50Mbps lines (Indonesia, Philippines, Vietnam) sit just above the 20Mbps 1080p figure, which is workable for a single stream and thin for parallel work. If you plan to run 1080p OBS with overlays, take the 200Mbps 2-core plans and leave the 50Mbps boxes to phone streaming.

## IP type does more for your result than core count

TikTok's risk scoring looks at the network the connection comes from. IP ranges owned by hosting companies grade poorly; residential and native commercial broadband ranges grade well. A 4-core machine on a flagged datacentre subnet will get throttled sooner than a 1-core box on a clean residential range, which is why the cheap-looking high-spec offers are often the worse buy for this specific workload.

Shared IPs add a second problem. When dozens of accounts sit behind one address, the nominal 10Mbps is being split at peak times, and if one user's account gets flagged the whole range carries the association. The working rule in the operator community is one IP per live account: need five accounts live at once, run five small instances rather than one bigger box.

That rule also explains the RAM tiers here. The 1-core/0.5GB plans (UK, Germany, Spain, France, Philippines, Thailand, and the two US dual-ISP segments) are fine for a proxy plus a single low-bitrate push. OBS 1080p encoding wants 2 cores and 1GB as a floor, so the AUD$12.90 US dual-ISP boxes are not the right pick for a desktop studio even though the port speed is generous.

## Who should not buy these plans

- **Five or more concurrent streams.** The 2-core/1GB, 10GB-disk shape is a single-account or small-batch environment. Split across multiple instances instead of stacking accounts on one.
- **Anyone who needs heavy rendering or browser automation.** Same reason: 10GB of disk and 1GB of RAM won't hold multiple automated browser profiles.
- **Buyers who want a long trial.** The refund window is 3 days or 10GB of traffic, whichever comes first. At 5Mbps, 10GB is roughly four hours of streaming, and less if traffic is counted in both directions. That is enough to confirm the IP geolocation and run one short test stream, not enough for load testing.
- **Non-relay users planning to run Shadowsocks.** It's prohibited on non-relay plans, which is stated in the plan notes rather than the sales copy.

Two more things to check before paying. Singapore residential stock is limited and can drop to zero during demand spikes, and published plan tables have shown more than one Singapore SKU with different IP tiers at different prices, so confirm which IP tier is actually in your cart. WePC bills in AUD with an optional CNY display; the cart's exchange rate is not necessarily the mid-market rate. Payment runs through PayPal, credit card or Alipay.

If you would rather not configure the proxy chain yourself, the vendor sells two non-refundable services: AUD$10 for a done-for-you deployment (relay server not included) and AUD$25 for guided setup training that covers relay configuration.
